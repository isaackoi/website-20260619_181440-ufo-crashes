## Introduction

Missing files are among the most frequently cited pieces of evidence in UFO crash debates. The argument is simple: if records related to an alleged crash have disappeared, perhaps they were removed to conceal the recovery of an alien spacecraft. [Official reviews]({{ 'official-reviews/' | relative_url }}), however, have consistently treated missing records more cautiously. Investigators generally distinguish between two separate claims: that records are incomplete, and that extraterrestrial technology was recovered. The first can sometimes be demonstrated. The second requires independent evidence. A gap in an archive may justify further scrutiny, but by itself it does not establish what the missing documents contained. Official reviews of alleged [UFO crashes]({{ 'ufo-crashes/' | relative_url }}) have repeatedly emphasised this distinction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-95-187.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nsiad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NSIAD-95-187 Government Records28 Jul 1995 — Our search for government records concerning the Roswell crash yielded two records origin...</span></span></span>

## Why Government Archives Can Be Incomplete

Many readers assume that government archives preserve every relevant document indefinitely. In practice, archives are shaped by retention schedules, administrative decisions, filing errors, accidental loss, and routine destruction policies.

Historical military [records]({{ 'records/' | relative_url }}) are especially vulnerable to gaps. During the mid-twentieth century, many records were not digitised, were stored in multiple locations, and were subject to destruction rules that would appear surprising today. Archivists regularly encounter missing correspondence, incomplete unit histories, lost logs, and destroyed message traffic across many subjects unrelated to UFOs. The existence of a missing file therefore does not automatically indicate deliberate concealment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/ufos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalarchives.gov.uk">[The National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The National ArchivesUFOsThis is a brief guide to researching records of UFOs. The surviving records consist mainly of documents relating...</span></span></span>

The problem is visible even in official UFO collections. National archives in both the United States and the United Kingdom describe their holdings as surviving records rather than complete records. Researchers are often working from what remained after decades of transfers, reorganisations, and disposal decisions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/ufos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalarchives.gov.uk">[The National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The National ArchivesUFOsThis is a brief guide to researching records of UFOs. This does not mean all missing records are unimportant. It means that incompleteness is a normal feature of historical archives and must be evaluated before extraordinary conclusions are drawn from it.

## The Roswell Example: Missing Records but No Direct Proof

The most famous case is Roswell. In response to congressional interest, the U.S. Government Accountability Office (GAO) conducted an extensive search for records related to the 1947 incident.

The GAO discovered that some Roswell Army Air Field administrative records and outgoing message files from the relevant era had been destroyed. It also reported uncertainty about who authorised the destruction and under what authority it occurred. These findings are often cited as [evidence]({{ 'evidence/' | relative_url }}) of a cover-up.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-95-187.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[gao.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nsiad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NSIAD-95-187 Government Records28 Jul 1995 — Our search for government records concerning the Roswell crash yielded two records origin...</span></span></span>

However, the GAO did not conclude that the missing records contained evidence of alien recovery. Instead, it reported the fact of the destruction and continued searching for other documentation. The review located surviving records, examined archival practices, and assessed reporting requirements, but it did not find documentary evidence demonstrating the recovery of extraterrestrial craft or [bodies]({{ 'bodies/' | relative_url }}).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-95-187.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nsiad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NSIAD-95-187 Government Records28 Jul 1995 — Our search for government records concerning the Roswell crash yielded two records origin...</span></span></span>

This distinction is crucial. The destruction of records creates uncertainty about what can be known. It does not establish the content of the destroyed material. A missing folder could have contained evidence supporting extraordinary claims, ordinary administrative correspondence, or nothing relevant at all. The absence of the documents leaves the question unanswered rather than resolved.

## How Reviewers Treat Gaps and Contradictions

Official investigators generally apply the same evidentiary principle used in other historical inquiries: conclusions should be based on what can be demonstrated, not on what might have existed.

When reviewers encounter missing records, they typically ask:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* Are there alternative sources covering the same events?
* Do surviving records contradict or support the claim?
* Are witness accounts consistent with one another?
* Is there physical evidence connected to the alleged incident?
* Is there a documented chain of custody for recovered materials?

</div>

A missing record may increase uncertainty, but it does not automatically outweigh surviving evidence. In many investigations, historians reconstruct events using parallel archives, financial records, personnel files, newspaper reports, memoranda, or correspondence from other agencies. The loss of one category of documents does not necessarily make an event unknowable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-95-187.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nsiad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NSIAD-95-187 Government Records28 Jul 1995 — Our search for government records concerning the Roswell crash yielded two records origin...</span></span></span>

This approach explains why official reviews can acknowledge archival gaps while still declining to endorse alien-recovery claims. The evidentiary threshold for proving the existence of recovered extraterrestrial technology is much higher than demonstrating that some records are missing.

## When Absence Becomes Suspicion but Not Proof

Missing files do have investigative significance. If records disappear at a critical moment, researchers naturally ask why. Suspicion can be reasonable, especially when destruction appears irregular or poorly documented.

The Roswell records identified by the GAO illustrate this point. The unexplained destruction of certain files remains a legitimate historical question and continues to attract attention. Yet suspicion and proof are not interchangeable. Even if one assumes that records were intentionally removed, a further leap is required to conclude that the motive was concealment of alien technology. Numerous other explanations would remain possible, including protection of classified military programmes, bureaucratic error, or routine but poorly documented disposal.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sgp.fas.org/othergov/roswell.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sgp.fas.org">[fas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sgp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FAS Project on Government SecrecyGAO Report on Roswell, NM UFO CrashThe document disposition form does not indicate what organization or...</span></span></span>

Investigators therefore treat missing records as a reason to keep looking rather than as a substitute for evidence. The gap itself may justify additional archival [searches]({{ 'searches/' | relative_url }}), interviews, or declassification efforts. It does not establish what the missing evidence would have shown.

## Why the Burden of Proof Matters

The logic underlying official reviews is straightforward: extraordinary claims require positive evidence, not merely gaps in the record.

A claim that an alien craft was recovered implies the existence of recoverable artefacts, laboratory analyses, transportation records, security procedures, personnel involvement, and administrative documentation. Even if some records are missing, investigators expect at least some corroborating evidence to survive elsewhere if such an operation occurred on a significant scale. The challenge is not simply finding missing files but finding verifiable evidence that independently supports the recovery claim.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nsa.gov/portals/75/documents/news-features/declassified-documents/ufo/report_af_roswell.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsa.gov">[nsa.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">report af roswell</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">report of air force research regarding the21 Jul 1994 — The &quot;Roswell Incident&quot; refers to an event that supposedly happened in July, 19...</span></span></span>

Recent government reviews have continued to apply this standard. Investigations examining decades of UFO and UAP claims have reported that they found no verifiable evidence of recovered extraterrestrial craft despite reviewing classified and unclassified records and interviewing individuals who alleged such programmes existed. ## What Missing Records Actually Tell Us

Missing records can reveal weaknesses in archival systems, record-management practices, or historical transparency. They can justify further investigation and occasionally expose procedural failures. What they do not do, on their own, is establish the existence of an alien spacecraft recovery programme.

The central lesson from official reviews is that uncertainty is not evidence. A destroyed file may leave an important question unanswered, but it cannot by itself answer that question.
