## Introduction

For researchers investigating alleged UFO crash recoveries, the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) is often treated as a route to hidden government [records]({{ 'records/' | relative_url }}). In practice, FOIA is an access law with limits, not a guarantee that every surviving document will be released. Even if a crash-related file exists, agencies may legally withhold it in full, release only fragments, or confirm that records were located while refusing to disclose their contents. The key reason is that FOIA contains nine statutory exemptions that allow information to remain secret under specific circumstances. In disputes over [UFO crash records]({{ 'foia-requests/' | relative_url }}), the most important questions are often not whether records exist, but whether they fall within exemptions for classification, other secrecy laws, privacy protection, law enforcement interests, or privileged internal communications. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.foia.gov/faq.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: foia.gov">[FOIA.gov+2National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">foia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Exemption 1: Information that is classified to protect national security. ## The Nine Exemption Problem for Crash Researchers

A common misunderstanding in UFO-records debates is that a successful [FOIA]({{ 'foia/' | relative_url }}) search must result in public disclosure. Federal agencies are required to search for responsive records, but they are not required to release information covered by one of FOIA's nine exemptions. As a result, a researcher may receive hundreds of pages with extensive redactions, or even a complete withholding notice, despite the existence of responsive material. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.foia.gov/faq.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: foia.gov">[FOIA.gov+2Department of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">foia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Exemption 1: Information that is classified to protect national security. * **Exemption 7** can also shield law-enforcement records, confidential sources, and investigative techniques. ## Classification Versus Ordinary Withholding

### Why Exemption 1 Receives So Much Attention

Among UFO researchers, Exemption 1 attracts the most interest because it covers information that has been properly classified in the interests of national defence or foreign policy. If a document contains classified operational information, intelligence capabilities, sensor systems, military technologies, or foreign-intelligence material, agencies may withhold it even decades after its creation if classification rules still apply. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov/archives/oip/foia-guide-2004-edition-exemption-1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov">[Department of Justice+2Department of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">foia guide 2004 edition exemption 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department of JusticeFOIA Guide, 2004 Edition: Exemption 1Exemption 1 of the FOIA integrates the national security protections provided b...</span></span></span>

Importantly, Exemption 1 does not require the government to prove that a UFO crash occurred. It merely allows information to remain secret if the information itself is properly classified. In a crash-related context, agencies could theoretically release the fact that a search occurred while continuing to redact technical details, collection methods, or intelligence sources. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov/archives/oip/foia-guide-2004-edition-exemption-1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov">[Department of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">foia guide 2004 edition exemption 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department of JusticeFOIA Guide, 2004 Edition: Exemption 1Exemption 1 of the FOIA integrates the national security protections provided b...</span></span></span>

### Exemption 3 Can Be Even Harder to Challenge

Researchers often focus on classification while overlooking Exemption 3. This exemption incorporates [secrecy]({{ 'secrecy/' | relative_url }}) requirements found in other federal laws. If another statute mandates nondisclosure, agencies may withhold information regardless of whether it is formally classified. Intelligence-community records are a frequent example because specialised secrecy statutes can independently block disclosure. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.foia.gov/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: foia.gov">[FOIA.gov+2FOIA.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">foia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">FOI A.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Freedom of Information ActExemption 3 incorporates into the FOIA certain nondisclosure provisions that are contained in other federal law...</span></span></span>

In practical terms, a requester may win an argument that material is no longer properly classified yet still face an Exemption 3 barrier if another law protects the same information. Courts have repeatedly recognised that Exemptions 1 and 3 can operate together, making certain records especially difficult to obtain. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.foia.gov/iso/opa/foia/oip-101012.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: foia.gov">[FOIA.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">foia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">oip 101012</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Summaries of New Decisions -- August 2010Exemption 1: The court finds that information properly withheld under Exemption... information...</span></span></span>

### Internal Deliberations and Legal Advice

Even where national security is not involved, Exemption 5 can shield drafts, legal analyses, recommendations, and internal policy discussions. For crash-related inquiries, this can affect later reviews, investigations, or agency debates about how to respond to public claims. ## How Redactions Differ From Missing Records

One of the most important distinctions in UFO document research is the difference between a redacted record and a missing record.

A **redacted record** is [evidence]({{ 'evidence/' | relative_url }}) that the agency located something responsive. The agency is effectively saying, "We found material, but part of it cannot be released." Modern declassification and FOIA systems often mark redactions with exemption codes that identify the legal basis for withholding specific passages. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/declassification/iscap/redaction-codes.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">redaction codes</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesRedaction Codes13 May 2025 — Each redaction will be associated with a redaction code, which gives the reason for why the...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 2025</span></span></span>

A **missing record**, by contrast, may indicate many different things:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* The record never existed.
* It existed but was destroyed under records-management procedures.
* It was misfiled or transferred elsewhere.
* The search did not reach the correct office or archive.
* The agency genuinely could not locate it.

</div>

A missing file therefore tells researchers much less than a heavily redacted file. Redactions confirm the existence of responsive material; [missing records]({{ 'missing-records/' | relative_url }}) often leave the underlying question unresolved. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/information-management/manage-information/selection-and-transfer/sensitivity-reviews-on-selected-records/access-to-public-records/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalarchives.gov.uk">[The National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The National ArchivesAccess to public recordsThe Information Commissioner&#x27;s Office (ICO) issues guidance on FOIA exemptions (the reasons...</span></span></span>

This distinction is frequently blurred in UFO discussions. A blacked-out page can be interpreted as proof of concealment, while a missing page can be interpreted as proof of destruction. Legally, however, the two situations are very different. One involves acknowledged records being partially withheld; the other involves uncertainty about whether records are available at all.

## What FOIA Exemptions Can and Cannot Prove

FOIA exemptions are often cited in arguments about UFO crash secrecy, but their existence does not resolve the underlying question of whether any particular crash claim is true.
